# LLM->human boundary: psycholinguistics and reading science

Field slice: surprisal/predictability, uniform information density (UID), lexical/syntactic entropy, homogeneity, reading-time/eye-tracking on machine text, cognitive load, memory/retention, fluency illusion, discourse coherence processing.

---

- **Hale, J. "A probabilistic Earley parser as a psycholinguistic model." NAACL 2001. https://doi.org/10.3115/1073336.1073357.** Foundational: derives word-by-word processing difficulty from the change in a probabilistic parser's state, formalizing what later becomes "surprisal." Method is computational/formal, not a human experiment; establishes the prefix-probability framework subsequent surprisal-reading-time work depends on. Relevant to the LLM->human question as the mechanism explaining why any predictable-vs-unpredictable text (LLM or human) should differ in processing cost. open-access: unknown.

- **Levy, R. "Expectation-based syntactic comprehension." Cognition, 2008. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cognition.2007.05.006.** Foundational surprisal paper: processing difficulty at a word is proportional to its negative log-probability given context (surprisal), derived from an information-theoretic resource-allocation argument. Explains garden-path effects, disambiguation costs, and expectation-based facilitation with one metric. Sets the theoretical basis for predicting that lower-surprisal (more predictable, e.g. LLM-typical) text should read faster/easier than higher-surprisal human text, all else equal. open-access: yes, https://www.mit.edu/~rplevy/papers/levy-2008-cognition.pdf.

- **Jaeger, T.F. "Redundancy and reduction: Speakers manage syntactic information density." Cognitive Psychology, 2010. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.cogpsych.2010.02.002.** Foundational UID paper: speakers choose reduced/expanded syntactic forms (e.g. optional "that") to keep information density roughly uniform across an utterance, avoiding both very high (costly) and very low (wasteful) local surprisal. Corpus + production study of English relative clauses/complementizers. Gives the human-production baseline against which LLM output's flatter local-surprisal profile (see Basani & Chen below) can be read as either "very UID-compliant" or as a homogenization artifact rather than genuine communicative efficiency. open-access: yes (author site PDF).

- **Levy, R., Jaeger, T.F. "Speakers optimize information density through syntactic reduction." NeurIPS 19, 2007. https://doi.org/10.7551/mitpress/7503.003.0111.** Companion foundational UID paper, corpus study showing reduction choices track a smoothing of information density. Same relevance as above: establishes UID as a property of human language production against which generated text can be benchmarked. open-access: unknown.

- **Gibson, E., Futrell, R., Piantadosi, S.T., Dautriche, I., Mahowald, K. et al. "How Efficiency Shapes Human Language." Trends in Cognitive Sciences, 2019. https://doi.org/10.1016/j.tics.2019.02.003.** Review tying UID, surprisal, and dependency-length minimization together as convergent pressures for communicative efficiency in human language. Not LLM-specific, but is the standard reference for what "efficient/optimal" human language production looks like, i.e., the yardstick used by papers below that ask whether LLM text is more/less uniform than this human optimum. open-access: unknown.

- **Bolliger, L., Haller, P., Cretton, D., Reich, D., Kew, T. et al. "EMTeC: A corpus of eye movements on machine-generated texts." Behavior Research Methods, 2025 (also arXiv:2408.04289, 2024). https://doi.org/10.3758/s13428-025-02677-4.** Naturalistic eye-tracking corpus: 107 native-English readers reading texts from 3 LLMs (Phi-2, Mistral-7B, WizardLM-13B) under 5 decoding strategies (greedy, beam, ancestral/top-k/top-p sampling), 42 prompts x 14 model/strategy combos = 588 stimulus conditions, 2000 Hz eye-tracker plus model-internal transition scores/attention/hidden states released alongside reading measures. Key finding: reading measures show minimal variation across generating models and strategies, i.e. readers' eye-movement signatures are largely insensitive to which model/decoding produced the text, in contrast to the large individual/item variation typically seen with human-authored text corpora. This is the field's first purpose-built instrument for the LLM->human eye-tracking question and a strong candidate baseline for comparison studies. open-access: yes, https://arxiv.org/pdf/2408.04289.

- **Gruteke Klein, K., Meiri, Y., Shubi, O., Berzak, Y. "The Effect of Surprisal on Reading Times in Information Seeking and Repeated Reading." arXiv, 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.08162.** Eye-tracking study relating LLM-derived surprisal to human reading times under information-seeking vs. regular reading and under repetition; finds systematic misalignments between how humans and LMs weight task-relevant information. Relevant less as an LLM-vs-human-text comparison than as evidence that the surprisal-reading-time link itself is task-modulated, which matters for interpreting any LLM-text reading-time study that doesn't control for reading purpose. open-access: yes.

- **Tsipidi, E., Nowak, F., Cotterell, R., Wilcox, E., Giulianelli, M., Warstadt, A. "Surprise! Uniform Information Density Isn't the Whole Story: Predicting Surprisal Contours in Long-form Discourse." arXiv, 2024. https://arxiv.org/abs/2410.16062.** Tests UID at the discourse level using LM-derived surprisal contours over long texts and finds local uniformity alone under-predicts the actual shape of surprisal trajectories in real (human) discourse; discourse-structural position matters. For the LLM->human question this sets an important caveat: "uniform" is not simply "flat" — a text (LLM or human) can fail to look human-like not by being flatter but by lacking the structured non-uniformity that discourse organization imposes. open-access: yes.

- **Basani, A.R., Chen, P.-Y. "Diversity Boosts AI-Generated Text Detection" (DivEye). arXiv, 2025. https://arxiv.org/abs/2509.18880.** Introduces a detector built on the finding that human-authored text has richer, more "rhythmic" fluctuation in local unpredictability across a passage, while LLM output keeps a more uniformly predictable probability trajectory token-to-token. Reports 18.7% detection-accuracy gains from surprisal-variance features over prior zero-shot detectors, i.e., quantifies the homogeneity-of-predictability gap directly. This is one of the more precise operationalizations of "LLM prose is locally too uniform" reviewed here, though it is a detection paper, not a reading-time/comprehension study. open-access: yes.

- **Shultz, T.R., Wise, E., Nobandegani, A.S. "Text understanding in GPT-4 versus humans." Royal Society Open Science, 2025. https://doi.org/10.1098/rsos.241313.** Compares GPT-4 to 40 neurotypical adults on the Discourse Comprehension Test (11 stories, 8 yes/no questions each; 5th-6th grade level) and on SAT/GRE/LSAT reading passages. Humans 93.05% vs GPT-4 96.59% on the simple test (not statistically different, p=.199); GPT-4 reaches 96-99th percentile on the harder standardized tests, i.e., its comprehension-test advantage grows with passage difficulty. Bears on the LLM->human boundary indirectly: it measures LLM reading comprehension of human text, the reverse direction, useful as a control showing the asymmetry is not simply "LLMs process worse." open-access: yes (PMC).

- **Britton, J., Cong, Y., Hsu, C.-T., Chersoni, E., Blache, P. et al. "On the influence of discourse connectives on the predictions of humans and language models." Frontiers in Human Neuroscience, 2024. https://doi.org/10.3389/fnhum.2024.1363120.** Self-paced reading (Italian n=133, Mandarin n=120) plus plausibility ratings on concessive/contrastive connectives signaling expectation reversal, compared to GPT-2 and Llama-2-7B surprisal. Humans show clear expectation-reversal effects from connectives (reading times track plausibility x connective interaction); GPT-2 partially reproduces the Chinese pattern but not the Italian one, and neither model shows a genuine expectation-reversal effect matching humans. Directly evidences a discourse-relation processing gap: LMs track local lexical statistics but miss connective-driven revision of expectations that human readers perform, a candidate mechanism for why LLM-generated argumentative/concessive text may read as subtly "flatter" in its discourse logic. open-access: yes (PMC).

- **Seals, S.M., Shalin, V.L. "Long-form analogies generated by chatGPT lack human-like psycholinguistic properties." arXiv, 2023. https://arxiv.org/abs/2306.04537.** Compares ChatGPT-generated long-form analogies to human ones on psycholinguistic norms (concreteness, imageability, familiarity, etc.) and finds the machine-generated analogies systematically lack the psycholinguistic richness of human ones. Directly relevant: a text-type where human writing is known to lean on rich imageable/concrete language, and LLM output measurably under-uses that register, a specific lexical mechanism for perceived "flatness." open-access: yes.

- **Guo, Y., Shang, G., Vazirgiannis, M., Clavel, C. "The Curious Decline of Linguistic Diversity: Training Language Models on Synthetic Text." NAACL Findings 2024 (also arXiv:2311.09807). https://doi.org/10.18653/v1/2024.findings-naacl.228.** Shows LM-generated text has lower lexical and syntactic diversity than human text, and that iteratively training on model output compounds the diversity loss (model collapse in diversity terms) across generations. Establishes, at corpus scale, the homogeneity property that later reading/detection studies treat as a given; also warns that as more LLM text re-enters training/reading ecosystems, the human-text baseline itself risks drifting toward the LLM's narrower distribution. open-access: yes, arXiv:2311.09807.

- **Muñoz-Ortiz, A., Gómez-Rodríguez, C., Vilares, D. "Contrasting Linguistic Patterns in Human and LLM-Generated News Text." Artificial Intelligence Review, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10462-024-10903-2.** Corpus comparison of human vs. multiple-LLM-generated news articles across lexical, syntactic, and psycholinguistic-norm features; LLM text shows narrower vocabulary, more auxiliary-verb use, fewer content words, simpler and more passive-leaning syntax than matched human articles. Gives a broad multi-feature confirmation of the lexical/syntactic homogeneity finding in a genre (news) where fluency illusion effects on readers are practically consequential. open-access: unknown.

- **Zamaraeva, O., Flickinger, D., Bond, F., Gómez-Rodríguez, C. "Comparing LLM-generated and human-authored news text using formal syntactic theory." ACL 2025 (arXiv:2506.01407). https://arxiv.org/abs/2506.01407.** Uses HPSG (Head-driven Phrase Structure Grammar) parsing rather than surface n-gram stats to compare syntactic structure of six LLMs' output against New York Times articles, finding systematic structural differences invisible to shallower metrics. Adds a formally grounded syntactic-entropy angle to the homogeneity literature: LLM sentences are not just lexically narrower but structurally patterned in ways a deep grammar can detect even when surface fluency is high. open-access: yes.

- **Zindulka, N.J., Goller, S., Fernandes, D., Welsch, R., Buschek, D. "The AI Memory Gap: Users Misremember What They Created With AI or Without." CHI 2026 (arXiv:2509.11851). https://doi.org/10.1145/3772318.3791494.** Pre-registered study, N=184: participants generated/elaborated ideas alone or with an LLM chatbot, then one week later tried to attribute authorship (self vs. AI) of each idea/text. Source-memory accuracy dropped sharply after any AI involvement, with the steepest drop in mixed human-AI workflows (idea from one source, elaboration from the other); computational source-memory modeling corroborates the effect. This is a direct memory/retention result for the LLM->human boundary, though it measures memory for authorship/origin of content one helped produce, rather than pure reading of someone else's LLM text; still the clearest "AI text degrades source memory" finding in the set. open-access: yes, arXiv:2509.11851.

- **Samardzic, K., Curkovic, M., Okmazic, F., Glumac, S., Vrdoljak, M. et al. "Patients Prefer Human Empathy, but Not Always Human Wording: A Single-Blind Within-Subject Trial of GPT-Generated vs. Clinician Discharge Texts in Emergency Ophthalmology." Clinics and Practice, 2025. https://doi.org/10.3390/clinpract15110208.** Single-blind within-subject trial where patients read paired GPT-generated and clinician-written discharge instructions without being told which was which. Patients rated perceived empathy higher for human-authored text even when they could not reliably identify authorship from wording alone, i.e., a felt-quality (warmth/empathy) gap survives even when the fluency/wording gap does not. Useful counterpoint to pure fluency-illusion findings: some receiver-side differences are about perceived intent/care, not processing ease. open-access: unknown.

- **Kumar, A., Mikayelyan, V., Vorfolomeyeva, K. "Fluency Illusion: A Review on Influence of ChatGPT in Classroom Settings." Information (MDPI), 2026. https://doi.org/10.3390/info17030299.** Narrative review of 41 publications (2022-early 2026) on ChatGPT's classroom effects; frames "fluency illusion" as clean syntax + confident tone + engaging narrative producing cognitive ease that readers/learners mistake for genuine understanding or profundity, and reports that evidence for durable conceptual learning after LLM-assisted study remains mixed despite reported gains in perceived understanding and efficiency. Directly names and reviews the metacognitive mechanism the question asks about (feeling of understanding vs. measured understanding) specifically in the context of reading AI output. open-access: yes.

- **Scantamburlo, R., Mezzanzana, M., Buonanno, G., Bertolotti, F. "Semantic Delta: An Interpretable Signal Differentiating Human and LLMs Dialogue." arXiv, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.19849.** Proposes a semantic-drift-based signal that separates human from LLM dialogue turns by how much meaning shifts turn-to-turn, interpretable rather than a black-box classifier. Adds a discourse-dynamics (rather than static lexical/syntactic) axis to the homogeneity picture: LLM dialogue shows a more constrained trajectory of semantic movement across turns than human dialogue. open-access: yes.

- **Inoshita, K., Omura, M., Yamanaka, T., Maeda, G., Tsuji, K. "Does AI Homogenize Student Thinking? A Multi-Dimensional Analysis of Structural Convergence in AI-Augmented Essays." arXiv, 2026. https://arxiv.org/abs/2603.21228.** Multi-dimensional structural analysis of student essays written with vs. without LLM assistance; finds AI-augmented essays converge structurally (argument organization, not just wording) across students who used the same tool, more than unaided essays converge with each other. Extends the homogeneity finding from single-text properties (entropy, lexical diversity) to across-author convergence, relevant to what a reader encountering many LLM-assisted texts experiences as sameness at the corpus level, not just within one text. open-access: yes.

## Synthesis: proposed mechanisms for the LLM->human reading difference

1. **Lower local surprisal / higher predictability.** LLM decoding (esp. greedy/low-temperature) concentrates probability mass, producing tokens that are individually easier to predict than human tokens in the same context — Basani & Chen 2025; Guo et al. 2024; Muñoz-Ortiz et al. 2024. Surprisal theory (Hale 2001; Levy 2008) predicts this should read as locally *easier*, i.e. faster, lower-effort processing per word.
2. **But the ease may be too uniform, not properly UID-shaped.** Human UID (Jaeger 2010; Levy & Jaeger 2007; Gibson et al. 2019) is a structured, discourse-aware smoothing of density, not flatness; Tsipidi et al. 2024 shows discourse position modulates real surprisal contours, and LLM output may lack that structure even while being locally low-surprisal. This is a candidate reason "smooth" LLM text can still feel subtly off to attentive readers.
3. **Reduced lexical/syntactic entropy at corpus scale.** Guo et al. 2024, Muñoz-Ortiz et al. 2024, and Zamaraeva et al. 2025 converge on narrower vocabulary, simpler/more passive syntax, and detectable structural regularities in LLM text versus matched human text — a distributional homogeneity independent of any single reader's processing.
4. **Empirically, first-pass reading behavior may not track the source at all.** EMTeC (Bolliger et al. 2025) found minimal variation in eye-movement measures across generating models/decoding strategies, suggesting the predicted "easier reading" from lower surprisal may not manifest as strongly in real eye-tracking data as corpus-level entropy differences would imply — an open tension in the field.
5. **Discourse-relation processing is where models under-reproduce human patterns most clearly.** Britton et al. 2024 shows LMs partially track connective-driven surprisal but miss human expectation-reversal effects; this is a specific, testable discourse-coherence gap between LLM output's statistical patterning and how humans actually revise expectations mid-sentence.
6. **Fluency is dissociable from comprehension and from felt quality.** Kumar et al. 2026 names the metacognitive mechanism (ease of processing read as understanding); Samardzic et al. 2025 shows a felt-empathy gap that survives blinding to authorship, meaning receiver-side effects are not reducible to surprisal/fluency alone.
7. **Reduced psycholinguistic richness in specific registers.** Seals & Shalin 2023 shows ChatGPT analogies lack the concreteness/imageability of human analogies — a lexical mechanism plausibly linked to weaker mental-imagery engagement and, downstream, to memorability.
8. **Memory for AI-touched content is measurably worse, at least for source attribution.** Zindulka et al. 2026 (N=184, pre-registered) is the strongest causal-leaning result here: AI involvement degrades source memory, worst in mixed human/AI workflows. This is adjacent to but not identical to "reading someone else's pure-LLM text" — it is memory for content one helped produce.
9. **Homogeneity compounds across authors and across generations.** Inoshita et al. 2026 (cross-student convergence) and Guo et al. 2024 (training-on-synthetic-text collapse) both point to a systemic risk: as more LLM text circulates, the human-text baseline used for comparison may itself drift toward the LLM's narrower distribution, complicating future measurement.
10. **The asymmetry is not simply "LLMs are worse at understanding human text."** Shultz et al. 2025 shows GPT-4 matches or exceeds human comprehension-test performance on human-written passages, so the receiver-side effects catalogued above are specific to *reading LLM output*, not a general LLM-vs-human capability gap.
