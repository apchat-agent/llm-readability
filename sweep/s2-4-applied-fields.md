# LLM->human boundary: applied and adjacent fields (receiving-side studies)

Field slice: translation studies, education, medicine/health communication, legal/technical writing, journalism, software engineering, customer support/negotiation, accessibility/plain language, and objective-outcome comparisons. Search date 2026-09-15.

## Translation studies

- **Guerberof-Arenas A, Toral A. "The impact of post-editing and machine translation on creativity and reading experience." Translation Spaces, 2020. https://doi.org/10.1075/ts.20035.gue.** A fictional story was translated EN->Catalan three ways (MT, post-edited MTPE, unaided human HT); 88 readers then read one version each and were measured on reading experience and creativity perception. MT and MTPE versions scored lower on reading enjoyment/creativity than HT despite comparable technical quality. Shows the reception gap survives even when the MT output is post-edited to fluency — the *process* that produced the text, not just its surface correctness, affects how it lands.
  open-access: unknown, no PDF found.

- **Doherty S, O'Brien S, Carl M. "Eye tracking as an MT evaluation technique." Machine Translation, 2010. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10590-010-9070-9.** Establishes eye-tracking (fixation count/duration, gaze) as a way to measure reader cognitive effort on raw MT output versus human text, rather than relying on subjective ratings. Foundational method paper for the "objective reader burden" line of MT reception research. Argues effort during reading, not just post-hoc quality judgment, is the right dependent variable for evaluating MT for a receiving reader.
  open-access: unknown.

- **Koponen M. "Is machine translation post-editing worth the effort? A survey of research into post-editing and effort." The Journal of Specialised Translation, 2016. https://doi.org/10.26034/cm.jostrans.2016.303.** Survey synthesizing process/product studies of post-editing effort (temporal, technical, cognitive) across the 2000s-2010s MT literature. Concludes effort measures diverge from simple error counts — a text can look "correct" on an error typology yet still cost a reader/editor more processing effort than human-authored text. Useful bridge between production-side post-editing-effort research and reception-side reading-effort research.
  open-access: yes. PDF: https://www.jostrans.org/issue25/art_koponen.pdf

- **Vieira LN. "Indices of cognitive effort in machine translation post-editing." Machine Translation, 2014. https://doi.org/10.1007/s10590-014-9156-x.** Combines eye-tracking, keystroke logging and retrospective think-aloud on professional post-editors working with MT output. Finds effort indices only partially correlate with automatic MT quality metrics (e.g., BLEU), meaning the receiving human's actual processing cost is not well predicted by the metrics usually used to certify MT output as "good enough." Direct evidence that objective machine-scored quality and human processing cost are different axes.
  open-access: unknown.

## Medicine and health communication

- **Zaretsky J, Kim JM, Baskharoun S, Zhao Y, Austrian J, Aphinyanaphongs Y, Gupta R, Blecker S, Feldman J. "Generative Artificial Intelligence to Transform Inpatient Discharge Summaries to Patient-Friendly Language and Format." JAMA Network Open, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1001/jamanetworkopen.2024.0357.** GPT-4 rewrote clinical discharge notes into patient-friendly language; clinician reviewers and readability metrics assessed the outputs against the original clinician-written notes. LLM-simplified notes scored substantially more readable while clinicians judged accuracy largely preserved, but with a nontrivial rate of omissions/inaccuracies needing correction. Direct evidence that LLM mediation trades readability gains against new, LLM-specific accuracy risk on the receiving patient's copy.
  open-access: yes (JAMA Network Open is OA). PDF: https://jamanetwork.com/journals/jamanetworkopen/fullarticle/2816174

- **Bert F, Rosset M, Conrado E, Consoli L, Scaioli G, Lo Moro G, Siliquini R. "Comprehension of an AI-generated discharge letter versus a traditional discharge letter: two controlled quasi-experimental parallel-group studies." International Journal for Quality in Health Care, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1093/intqhc/mzag133.** Two parallel-group quasi-experiments comparing patient comprehension of an AI-generated vs a standard clinician-written discharge letter, using comprehension scoring as the outcome (not preference). Framed explicitly around the documented problem that patients misunderstand standard discharge letters, driving medication errors and readmissions. One of the few studies with comprehension (not satisfaction) as the primary endpoint for LLM-mediated clinical text.
  open-access: unknown.

- **Xu et al. (15 authors). "Rethinking Pediatric Asthma Education Through Large Language Model Generation and Simplification: Randomized Double-Blind Study." Journal of Medical Internet Research, 2026. https://doi.org/10.2196/98118.** Randomized double-blind trial of LLM-generated/simplified pediatric asthma education materials vs standard materials, measuring caregiver comprehension outcomes rather than ratings. Tests whether LLM simplification of health content changes actual uptake of information, with blinding to reduce the "I was told it's AI" confound. Directly targets the field's central methods problem: separating text-quality effects from source-label effects.
  open-access: yes (JMIR is OA). PDF: https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e98118/PDF

- **Seo, Yook, Kim, Lee, Yoo, Byeon, Choi. "Large Language Model-Based Simplification of Digital Therapeutics Explanations for Insomnia and Nicotine Dependence: Two Randomized Online Experiments." JMIR Human Factors, 2026. https://doi.org/10.2196/89451.** Two randomized online experiments testing LLM-simplified explanations of digital-therapeutic mechanisms against standard explanations, for comprehension and behavioral intention (uptake), not just liking. Targets patient education material that is normally too technical for general readers. Shows LLM simplification can move an objective downstream behavioral-intention measure, not only self-reported understanding.
  open-access: yes. PDF: https://humanfactors.jmir.org/2026/1/e89451/PDF

- **Holderried F, Sonanini S, Stegemann-Philipps C, Herrmann-Werner A, Spitzer M, Guthoff M, Heyne N, Sering K, Holderried M, Eisinger V. "Impact of GPT-4-Generated Discharge Letters on Patients' Medical Comprehension: Prospective Crossover Study." Journal of Medical Internet Research, 2026. https://doi.org/10.2196/81243.** Crossover design where the same patients read both a GPT-4-generated and a standard discharge letter, with comprehension as the primary outcome, grounded explicitly in cognitive load theory (dense clinician register overloads working memory). Within-subject design controls for reader ability, isolating the text-source effect on comprehension. One of the cleanest designs in this slice for isolating LLM-vs-human authorship as the manipulated variable.
  open-access: yes. PDF: https://www.jmir.org/2026/1/e81243/PDF

- **Serna, Yu, Diaz, Sakiyama, Ye, Rustagi, Sohn. "Self-reported comprehension of large language model-generated summaries of lung cancer screening reports: a vignette survey." Radiology Advances, 2026. https://doi.org/10.1093/radadv/umag008.** Vignette survey testing whether LLM-generated patient-friendly summaries of radiology reports reduce literacy-based comprehension disparities, motivated by patients now getting immediate portal access to raw radiology reports (which causes anxiety/misunderstanding). Measures self-reported comprehension across literacy strata rather than a single aggregate score. Suggests LLM mediation's benefit may be uneven — larger for lower-literacy readers — rather than a flat shift.
  open-access: unknown.

## Education

- **Escalante J, Pack A, Barrett A. "AI-generated feedback on writing: insights into efficacy and ENL student preference." International Journal of Educational Technology in Higher Education, 2023. https://doi.org/10.1186/s41239-023-00425-2.** Compares AI-generated (ChatGPT) feedback on student writing against instructor feedback, measuring both efficacy (whether students act on/incorporate the feedback) and preference, for English-as-additional-language students. Finds students could act on AI feedback about as well as on instructor feedback, but preference and perceived legitimacy diverged from actual efficacy. Important because it separates "students say they trust it" from "students actually use it correctly" — the efficacy/preference gap is the crux of the LLM->human question.
  open-access: yes. PDF: https://educationaltechnologyjournal.springeropen.com/counter/pdf/10.1186/s41239-023-00425-2.pdf

- **Wu R, Yu Z. "Do AI chatbots improve students learning outcomes? Evidence from a meta-analysis." British Journal of Educational Technology, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1111/bjet.13334.** Meta-analysis pooling experimental studies (k reported in-paper) of AI chatbot use on measured learning outcomes (not satisfaction), finding a positive but moderated effect, larger for higher-order skills and shorter interventions. Aggregates the outcome-based (as opposed to preference-based) literature on students receiving/interacting with AI-generated instructional content. Useful as the meta-level anchor for claims about learning-outcome effects of LLM-authored educational material.
  open-access: unknown.

## Journalism

- **Graefe A, Haim M, Haarmann B, Brosius HB. "Readers' perception of computer-generated news: Credibility, expertise, and readability." Journalism, 2018. https://doi.org/10.1177/1464884916641269.** Online experiment (N=986) with a 2x2x2 design varying topic and both actual and *declared* source (human vs computer) of news articles, rating credibility, readability and journalistic expertise. Actual authorship barely affected ratings, but declared authorship did — readers rated computer-labeled articles as less well-written even when the text was identical. Classic demonstration that source-label effects can swamp actual-content effects in LLM/automation reception.
  open-access: unknown.

- **Wölker A, Powell TE. "Algorithms in the newsroom? News readers' perceived credibility and selection of automated journalism." Journalism, 2021 (print; online first 2018). https://doi.org/10.1177/1464884918757072.** Experimental study of how knowledge of automated authorship affects article selection and credibility judgments among news readers. Extends the credibility-perception literature to actual reading/selection behavior rather than only post-hoc ratings. Part of the core automated-journalism reception corpus this slice draws on.
  open-access: unknown.

- **Graefe A, Bohlken N. "Automated Journalism: A Meta-Analysis of Readers' Perceptions of Human-Written in Comparison to Automated News." Media and Communication, 2020. https://doi.org/10.17645/mac.v8i3.3019.** Meta-analysis of 12 experimental/descriptive studies (N=4,473 total) comparing reader perceptions of automated vs human-written news on credibility, quality and readability. Found no overall credibility difference, but a small human-written advantage on quality/readability judgments. The field's aggregate answer for "does the reader treat machine-written news differently," at the perception (not comprehension) level.
  open-access: unknown.

- **Waddell TF. "Can an Algorithm Reduce the Perceived Bias of News? Testing the Effect of Machine Attribution on News Readers' Evaluations of Bias, Anthropomorphism, and Credibility." Journalism & Mass Communication Quarterly, 2019. https://doi.org/10.1177/1077699018815891.** Online experiment testing whether attributing a news article to an algorithm (vs a human journalist) changes readers' perceived bias, anthropomorphism, and credibility judgments, using the MAIN model framework. Attribution to a machine reduced perceived bias in some conditions — the opposite direction from the "AI text is less trusted" intuition found elsewhere, suggesting the effect is topic/valence-dependent, not a fixed universal discount.
  open-access: unknown.

- **Altay S, Gilardi F. "People are skeptical of headlines labeled as AI-generated, even if true or human-made, because they assume full AI automation." PNAS Nexus, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1093/pnasnexus/pgae403.** Two preregistered online experiments (N=4,976, US/UK) on how an "AI-generated" label changes perceived truth of headlines, independent of actual authorship and actual truth value. Labeling reduced perceived accuracy even for true, human-written headlines mislabeled as AI, because readers infer "fully automated, unverified" from the label itself. Shows the LLM->human gap can be a *labeling* effect decoupled entirely from the text's actual provenance or quality.
  open-access: yes (PNAS Nexus is OA). PDF: https://academic.oup.com/pnasnexus/article-pdf/3/10/pgae403/59700736/pgae403.pdf

- **Lermann Henestrosa A, Kimmerle J. "The Effects of Assumed AI vs. Human Authorship on the Perception of a GPT-Generated Text." Journalism and Media, 2024. https://doi.org/10.3390/journalmedia5030069.** Between-groups experiment (N=734) with a single GPT-generated text on a complex topic, where only the *labeled* authorship (AI vs human) was manipulated. Prior literature (mostly simple topics) found no credibility gap by authorship label; this study on a complex topic did find one, suggesting topic difficulty moderates whether the authorship-label penalty appears. Reinforces that label effects and content effects need to be experimentally decoupled, and that findings from simple-topic studies may not generalize.
  open-access: unknown.

## Software engineering

- **Xiao T, Hata H, Treude C, Matsumoto K. "Generative AI for Pull Request Descriptions: Adoption, Impact, and Developer Interventions." Proceedings of the ACM on Software Engineering, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1145/3643773.** Empirical study of GitHub Copilot for Pull Requests in the wild: how often developers accept AI-generated PR descriptions as-is vs edit/intervene, and what that implies about trust and reading behavior. Uses actual developer edit behavior (an objective, observed outcome) rather than a stated-preference survey. Directly answers "do developers treat LLM-written PR text as needing more verification/editing than human-written text," with real repository data.
  open-access: unknown (ACM). Landing: https://dl.acm.org/doi/10.1145/3643773

## Clinical text summarization (LLM output judged by expert readers)

- **Van Veen D, Van Uden C, Blankemeier L, Delbrouck JB, Aali A, Bluethgen C, Pareek A, Polacin M, Reis EP, Seehofnerová A, Rohatgi N, Hosamani P, Collins W, Ahuja N, Langlotz CP, Hom J, Gatidis S, Pauly J, Chaudhari AS. "Adapted large language models can outperform medical experts in clinical text summarization." Nature Medicine, 2024. https://doi.org/10.1038/s41591-024-02855-5.** Blinded reader study where physicians evaluated LLM-generated vs physician-generated clinical summaries (radiology reports, progress notes, patient questions) without knowing the source, scoring completeness/correctness/conciseness. LLM summaries were judged noninferior or superior to human summaries by the blinded expert readers on most axes, with hallucinated content the main residual risk. Rare case in this sweep of an *objective, blinded, expert-reader* outcome directly comparing LLM- vs human-authored text on the same task — removes the authorship-label confound entirely.
  open-access: unknown (Nature Medicine, some content OA). Landing: https://www.nature.com/articles/s41591-024-02855-5

## Customer support (field productivity outcome)

- **Brynjolfsson E, Li D, Raymond LR. "Generative AI at Work." National Bureau of Economic Research Working Paper 31161, 2023. https://doi.org/10.3386/w31161.** Field study of a generative-AI conversational assistant deployed for customer-support agents at a software firm (~5,000 agents), using actual resolution-time and customer-satisfaction records rather than survey ratings. AI assistance raised productivity and customer satisfaction most for less-experienced agents, with the assistant effectively transferring the communication patterns of top performers. Not a pure LLM->human boundary study (agents remain in the loop, editing before customers see text) but the strongest available field-scale objective-outcome evidence for the customer-support slice; note as a mediated rather than direct LLM->human comparison.
  open-access: yes (NBER working papers are freely downloadable, registration only). PDF: https://www.nber.org/system/files/working_papers/w31161/w31161.pdf

## Synthesis: mechanisms this field slice proposes for why LLM->human differs

1. **Process, not just product, shapes reception.** Guerberof-Arenas & Toral (2020) show MT/MTPE text reads worse even when post-edited to fluency — origin leaves a trace readers respond to beyond surface correctness.
2. **Reading effort is a separate axis from error rate.** Doherty/O'Brien/Carl (2010), Koponen (2016), Vieira (2014) — cognitive/processing cost during reading does not track automatic quality scores or error counts; a "correct" MT text can still cost more to read.
3. **Label effects can dominate content effects.** Altay & Gilardi (2024), Graefe et al. (2018) — an "AI-generated" tag shifts perceived truth/quality even holding the text fixed, sometimes even for human-written or true content mislabeled as AI.
4. **Topic complexity moderates the label penalty.** Lermann Henestrosa & Kimmerle (2024) found an authorship penalty on a complex topic where prior simple-topic studies (Graefe 2018, Waddell 2019) found none — the LLM->human gap is not fixed, it interacts with content difficulty.
5. **Efficacy and stated preference diverge.** Escalante et al. (2023) — ENL students could act on AI feedback about as well as instructor feedback, but did not rate it as trustworthy; self-report and behavior disagree.
6. **Domain register overload compounds the gap.** Holderried et al. (2026), Zaretsky et al. (2024) — clinical text's technical density is the mechanism (cognitive load theory) that LLM simplification exploits; the receiver-side benefit is a readability/load reduction, not a truth-value change.
7. **Simplification benefit may be uneven across readers.** Serna et al. (2026) suggest LLM mediation narrows comprehension disparity more for lower-literacy readers than higher-literacy ones — an equity-shaped effect, not a flat shift.
8. **Blinding removes the label confound and can flip the result.** Van Veen et al. (2024) — blinded expert readers rated LLM clinical summaries as good or better than human ones; unblinded/labeled studies elsewhere show a penalty, implying much of the "gap" literature is measuring the label, not the text.
9. **Verification burden shows up in downstream behavior, not just ratings.** Xiao et al. (2024) — developer edit/intervention rates on AI PR descriptions are a behavioral trust signal, distinct from stated trust.
10. **Aggregate learning/behavior outcomes are positive but small and moderated.** Wu & Yu (2024), Seo et al. (2026) — meta-analytic and RCT evidence shows real (not just self-reported) outcome movement from LLM-authored educational/health content, but effect sizes are moderated by task type and delivery, not uniformly large.
