Abstract Background Patients access radiology reports without delay, which can cause anxiety and misunderstanding. While large language models (LLMs) can generate patient-friendly summaries (PS) to mitigate this, their potential to address literacy-based disparities remains unquantified. Purpose To measure the effect of LLM-generated PS on the objective comprehension and subjective experiences of individuals reading lung cancer screening computed tomography reports, and to determine the differential impact with respect to self-rated English and health literacy. Materials and Methods This cross-sectional survey (July 24–28, 2025) used a within-subjects design. Participants from the online research platform Prolific , self-enrolled from the general U.S. population, viewed 3 lung cancer screening reports (negative; negative with complex incidentals; suspicious for malignancy), first in their original format and then with an LLM-generated PS. Objective comprehension, subjective experiences (including anxiety, via a 5-point Likert scale), and hypothetical communication intent were assessed after each viewing. Univariate and multivariate analyses, including demographic subgroup comparisons, were performed. Results A total of 1815 participants (mean age, 46 years ± 16 [SD]; 919 women) who completed the survey were evaluated. The addition of a PS improved objective comprehension ( P &lt; .001) and reduced anxiety ( P &lt; .001) for all scenarios. This effect was most pronounced for respondents with low self-rated English literacy, who had greater comprehension gain ( P &lt; .001) and anxiety reduction ( P = .012) than those with high literacy. Individuals with low self-rated health literacy also experienced more anxiety reduction ( P &lt; .001). PS also increased the proportion of participants reporting that they would be willing to wait for a scheduled appointment to discuss their results ( P &lt; .001). Conclusion LLM-generated PS of lung cancer screening reports increase comprehension and reduce anxiety, most notably among individuals with lower self-rated English and health literacy. If validated in a patient population, they represent a potential tool to improve communication. Introduction The 21st Century Cures Act mandates patients’ access to their health information without delay, including radiology reports. Although this legislation promotes transparency, it also means patients may view highly technical reports even before their provider, which can cause anxiety 1 , 2 and misinterpretation. Although patients have generally welcomed the immediate release of results, 3 the presence of this communication gap can potentially undermine patient-clinician relationships and shared decision-making. 4 Patient-friendly summaries (PS) of radiology reports, which simplify technical jargon, represent a possible solution to improve patient comprehension and lessen anxiety. 5–7 The recent advent of large language models (LLMs) such as GPT-4 8 offers a promising, scalable way to generate PS automatically, with initial, smaller-scale studies already demonstrating that LLMs can simplify report readability and improve patient satisfaction. 5 , 9 , 10 It is unknown if LLM-generated summaries disproportionately benefit individuals with lower English or health literacy, which is a key question for advancing health equity. While patient-friendly materials are often assumed to be most helpful to patients with lower literacy, especially because only 4% of radiology reports are assessed as written at an eighth-grade reading level, 11 the specific impact on these individuals has not been rigorously measured. Moreover, it remains unclear what informational gaps persist for patients even after a PS is provided; understanding these gaps and ensuring clear communication are essential to promote patient engagement, which may influence follow-up in domains such as lung cancer screening. 12 Therefore, the purpose of this study was to measure the positive effects of LLM-generated PS on the objective comprehension and subjective experience of individuals reading a range of lung cancer screening reports, and to determine the differential impact on individuals with varying self-rated English and health literacy. Though the benefits of LLM-generated summaries might generalize across radiology, lung cancer screening reports were targeted because of their variable complexity via the Lung Imaging Reporting and Data System (Lung-RADS), frequency of potentially confusing incidental findings, and high potential for anxiety/distress. 13 We also sought to evaluate how the presence of PS could hypothetically affect a respondent’s behavioral intent regarding the timing of their next interaction with their clinician. Materials and methods Survey design and recruitment This cross-sectional survey was institutional review board–certified as Category II-Exempt (#25-44641). Participants were recruited July 24–28, 2025, via the online research platform Prolific ( https://www.prolific.com ). Eligibility criteria included U.S. residence, age ≥18 years, and ≥95% researcher approval rate for surveys taken by the participant. Prolific prescreening filters created a nationally representative sample for age, sex, and ethnicity based on U.S. census data. Participants were given a maximum of 1 hour to complete the 50-question survey. Survey participation was voluntary, with electronic informed consent obtained before initiation. Responses were deidentified and linked only to a ProlificID. Exclusion criteria included: incompletion, failing 2 attention check questions or voluntarily opting out, and providing low-effort responses (ie, answering “I don’t know” for all objective questions) ( Figure 1 ). Survey procedure The survey ( Supplemental Material ) was designed and hosted via Qualtrics ( https://www.qualtrics.com ), using a within-subjects, pre-post design. For 3 clinical scenarios, participants first viewed an original computed tomography (CT) report and answered a set of questions. Immediately after, they were shown the corresponding PS and answered an identical set of questions. Measured outcomes were compared before and after PS exposure. Question materials Three deidentified lung cancer screening CT reports were selected from an institutional database to reflect a diverse range of pathologies, incidental findings, and potential for patient anxiety: (1) a negative screen (Lung-RADS 1); (2) a report negative for lung cancer but with complex incidentals (Lung-RADS 2S); and (3) a report suspicious for lung malignancy requiring action (Lung-RADS 4B). For each original report ( Table S1 ), a corresponding PS was generated using an LLM (GPT-4o) via a Health Insurance Portability and Accountability Act–compliant institutional interface. 14 The prompt ( Table S2 ) was iteratively refined to optimize for structural consistency and layperson readability. Inspired by Tang et al, 10 this prompt used a few-shot technique, where examples guided the desired output; it was grounded with a knowledge base of common chest terminology, and used a temperature of 0.7 to balance factuality with natural prose. All PS were reviewed by a board-certified radiologist (J.H.S.) for accuracy. Each PS was generated in approximately 2–6 seconds. Measures ( supplemental material ) For each scenario, we assessed objective comprehension of the “main findings” and “next steps” with 2 multiple-choice questions. Respondents additionally rated their subjective ease of understanding, confidence in next steps, and anxiety level on a 5-point Likert scale (1 = very difficult/low, 5 = very easy/high). We assessed hypothetical behavioral intent regarding each report by asking respondents to indicate, both after viewing the original report and PS, whether they would call their clinician immediately (without guarantee of reaching them), wait for a scheduled appointment 1–2 weeks away, or do something else (free text). Finally, respondents were asked if they had persisting questions or desired additions for each PS. Respondent demographics including age, household income, and education level were collected ( Table 1 ). Additionally, respondents were asked about their health care work history (“Have you ever worked in health care?”, followed by open-ended specification of their role) and radiology report readership (“In the past year have you read 1 or more of your own radiology reports?”, followed by specification of resources used to aid understanding). Self-rated English literacy was assessed with the 5-point Likert scale question, “How comfortable are you reading complex written materials in English, such as legal documents or technical manuals?” Although not a clinically validated tool, similar self-reported measures are standard in demographics research (eg, U.S. Census) and others have demonstrated that single-item self-reports can serve as useful proxies for literacy in screening contexts. 15 Self-rated health literacy was assessed via the 5-point Likert scale question, “How confident are you in filling out medical forms by yourself?” 16 Subgroup categorizations are detailed in Table S3 . Statistical and thematic analysis A sample size of approximately 1800 participants was chosen to ensure sufficient statistical power (&gt;80%) to detect small effect sizes (Cohen’s d ≥ 0.1) for within-subjects comparisons, including for demographic subgroups. All quantitative analyses were performed using R (version 4.5.1; R Core Team 2025) with significance at P &lt; .05. For univariate analysis, McNemar’s chi-squared test was used for changes in objective comprehension and behavioral intent; Wilcoxon signed-rank tests were used for changes in Likert scale scores (ordinal data). For demographic subgroup analyses, the primary outcome variables were “comprehension gain” (the increase in total correct answers of 6) and “anxiety reduction” (the average decrease in Likert-scale anxiety points). Wilcoxon rank-sum tests and Kruskal-Wallis tests with Dunn’s post hoc testing were used for 2- and 3-subgroup comparisons, respectively. To assess the independent effects of literacy on these outcomes, 2 separate multivariate linear regressions were performed (primary predictor = self-rated English or health literacy), using covariates found to be significant in univariate analysis (age, radiology report readership, health care work history). The thematic analysis of open-ended responses was conducted via an LLM-assisted approach. OpenAI’s o3-mini model (January 2025 version; accessed August 2025) was used to randomly sample 100 responses and perform an initial inductive coding pass. Drafted thematic categories were reviewed by a human researcher for accuracy. Responses without improvement suggestions (ie, “none”) were excluded. Finally, the model coded 2501 eligible free-text comments into 7 thematic categories. Because responses could contain multiple themes, this yielded 3213 thematic assignments that served as the denominator for quantifying category frequency. Results Participant characteristics ( Table 1 ) Of 1990 participants who began the survey, 1815 were included in the final analysis (mean age, 46 years ± 16 [SD]; 919 women) ( Figure 1 ). The cohort was predominantly White/Caucasian (63.1%) and represented a wide range of household incomes (42.8% low income [$0–$60 000], 43.3% middle income [$60 000—$100 000]), 12.4% higher income [$150 000+]). Slightly more than half of the participants (57.6%) held a bachelor’s degree or higher. Regarding key experiential factors, most participants self-rated their English literacy (53.2%) and health literacy (68.3%) as high. A notable portion of the cohort had relevant medical context, with 21.7% of respondents self-identified as having worked in health care and 33.1% having read one of their own radiology reports within the past year. Of 601 participants who had read their own radiology report in the past year, 127 (21.1%) reported using an online large language model (eg, ChatGPT, Gemini, Claude) to help them understand it. Impact of patient-friendly summaries on objective comprehension and subjective experience The addition of an LLM-generated PS to the original CT reports resulted in a statistically significant improvement in objective comprehension across all three clinical scenarios ( Figure 2A ). For questions about the “Main Findings,” the percentage of respondents answering correctly increased from 94.8% to 99.2% for the Lung-RADS 1 report, from 78.3% to 87.2% for Lung-RADS 2S, and from 67.7% to 81.9% for Lung-RADS 4B ( P &lt; .001 for all). Similarly, for questions about “Next Steps,” the percentage of respondents answering correctly increased from 95.2% to 97.9% for the Lung-RADS 1 report, from 50.7% to 63.2% for Lung-RADS 2S, and from 43.7% to 63.7% for Lung-RADS 4B (all P &lt; .001). Subjective experience metrics also improved significantly with the addition of a PS across all 3 report scenarios ( P &lt; .001 for all comparisons, Figure 2B ). For each report, participants rated the PS as significantly easier to understand. For example, for the most technically complex report (Lung-RADS 2S), the mean ease of understanding score increased from 2.54 (“somewhat difficult” on the 5-point Likert scale) to 4.00 (“somewhat easy”). These shifts were greater in magnitude for the Lung-RADS 2S and 4B reports compared to Lung-RADS 1. Self-rated confidence in understanding of next steps similarly increased across all scenarios. Notably, the PS also resulted in statistically significant reductions in the anxiety respondents stated they would feel if they were patients, even for the Lung-RADS 4B scan suspicious for lung cancer. Differential impact of patient-friendly summaries across literacy-based subgroups Univariate analysis of outcomes by demographic subgroups revealed that the benefits of the PS were most pronounced among participants with lower self-rated English literacy ( Figure 3 ). The highest objective comprehension gain (mean [95% CI]) was observed for low self-rated English literacy (0.83 [0.69–0.98]) compared to those with middle (0.63 [0.55–0.72], P = .03) and high literacy (0.56 [0.50–0.63], P &lt; .001). This pattern was also seen with anxiety reduction (mean [95% CI]) for low (0.36 [0.29–0.43]) compared to middle (0.25 [0.21–0.29], P = .02) and high (0.26 [0.22–0.29], P = .01) self-rated English literacy. Similarly, individuals with low (0.45 [0.34–0.56], P &lt; . 001) or middle (0.34 [0.29–0.40], P = . 001) self-rated health literacy experienced greater reductions in anxiety compared to high literacy (0.22 [0.20–0.25]). Effect of patient-friendly summaries on hypothetical behavioral intent The improved understanding from the PS had a significant impact on participants’ hypothetical intended communication behavior with clinicians ( Figure 4 ). For all 3 reports, the summaries increased the percentage of participants who stated that, were they the patient receiving this report, they would wait for a scheduled appointment 1–2 weeks away rather than attempt to call their health care provider the same day (all P &lt; . 001). The magnitude of this shift varied by report complexity; it was most pronounced for Lung-RADS 2S (the nonmalignant yet most complex report) and lowest for Lung-RADS 4B. Discussion This exploratory survey observed that LLM-generated PS improved lung cancer screening report comprehension and reduced self-reported anxiety among nonpatient respondents presented with a hypothetical of undergoing a lung cancer screening patient experience. Our results suggest that, if confirmed in a patient population undergoing lung cancer screening, these tools may help reduce disparities in healthcare communication. The greatest benefits would be for individuals with low self-rated English ( P &lt; . 001 for comprehension gain, P = . 005 for anxiety reduction) and health literacy ( P &lt; . 001 for anxiety reduction) compared to high literacy. However, a communication gap remained, with respondents primarily desiring personalized risk assessment and clearer guidance on next-step urgency. Additionally, although PS improved comprehension for all Lung-RADS, approximately one-third of respondents still incorrectly identified the next steps for actionable findings (Lung-RADS 2S/4B), highlighting both the continued room for improvement in PS generation and continued importance of direct clinician communication to ensure patient safety. Our finding that LLM-generated PS improved respondent comprehension and self-reported anxiety is consistent with prior literature showing that LLMs can simplify radiology report readability and improve patient confidence. 9 , 17–21 However, unlike a recent systematic review that found lay summaries did not significantly reduce anxiety compared to traditional reports alone, 6 we found consistent anxiety reduction from PS among our nonpatient respondents, suggesting utility for investigating the benefit of this tool in real lung cancer screening populations. The disproportionate benefit of PS for individuals with lower self-rated English and health literacy warrants further investigation in clinical settings. Although our results support already established health care communication principles 22–24 and literature demonstrating improvements in subjective 5 , 25 , 26 and objective 21 , 27 report comprehension from PS, they additionally provide preliminary evidence that LLM-generated PS may mitigate literacy-based disparities in the interpretation of complex imaging reports. However, because our cohort was skewed toward highly educated and technologically literate individuals who have not necessarily undergone screening themselves, our findings should only be viewed as generating a hypothesis for how LLMs may impact health equity. Regarding hypothetical behavioral intent, the provision of a PS increased the proportion of participants stating that, were they the patient receiving the report, they would be willing to wait for a scheduled appointment to discuss findings. Notably, this effect persisted even for the Lung-RADS 4B report; this may reflect either a genuine positive effect of clarity on relieving anxiety or, alternatively, raises questions about how meaningfully respondents engaged with the scenarios and represents a limitation of hypothetical survey methodology in capturing real emotional responses to serious diagnoses. If replicated and validated prospectively in real patients with lung cancer risk, then examination of the effect of LLM-generated PS on unscheduled communications would be worth investigating. 2 , 28 , 29 Although LLM-generated PS were considered overwhelmingly helpful by our respondents, they also desired interpretation in addition to translation, most commonly as personalized risk assessment and next step guidance. Because more than 20% of respondents who had read their own reports reported using LLMs for interpretation, a possible future direction for LLM-generated PS may be to integrate understandable language with personalized information, along with validation in populations of patients with cancer. Notably, our PS generation took seconds and required only minor edits by a radiologist (primarily for tone consistency rather than factual correction), highlighting LLM scalability to produce PS. Nevertheless, navigating the risk of model hallucination will be a critical consideration if LLMs are tasked with providing guidance beyond translation. 30 This study had several limitations. First, participants recruited from an online platform may not be representative of a population undergoing lung cancer screening, limiting the generalizability of our results. Although our cohort was economically diverse, respondents were more educated and technologically literate (including with AI tools) than the U.S average. 31 Moreover, in our cohort, only 14.9% of respondents self-rated as low-English-literacy, limiting the ability to extrapolate about benefits in truly low-literacy populations. Second, our study lacked longitudinal context (eg, follow-up scans with stable findings), which is a major determinant of real-world anxiety. Third, the hypothetical nature of the survey could only assess informational comprehension and may not capture the psychological context and decision-making of real patients; behavioral intent particularly may differ from real-world communication behaviors. Therefore, these findings must be validated in prospective, patient-based studies to confirm whether improvements persist in real lung cancer screening populations facing emotional stress. Fourth, the lengths of reports may have confounded response correctness if participants grew tired while progressing through the survey. Alternatively, the fixed report presentation order may have introduced order effects, with performance improvements on subsequent reports due to repeated exposure rather than due to the PS itself. A counterbalanced design would have limited the effects of either fatigue or practice and strengthened causal inference. Fifth, English and health literacy were assessed with single, self-reported questions, which are subject to social-desirability bias and ceiling effects. Although our question for health literacy has been validated against established instruments, 16 our question for English literacy has not been validated as objectively related to this measure. Similarly, comprehension was assessed with only two unvalidated multiple-choice questions per report. Finally, we used a specific LLM prompt and tested specific clinical scenarios; although the scenarios captured a diversity of Lung-RADS, incidental findings, and clinical severity, this study’s positive results may not be generalizable to all LLM outputs or radiological contexts. LLM-generated PS of lung cancer screening CT reports improved comprehension and reduced self-reported anxiety among non-patient survey respondents, especially with lower self-rated English and health literacy. If validated in real-world patient populations, they represent a potential tool to improve healthcare communication.
