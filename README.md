# llm-readability

Why LLM output is hard for humans to read: state of the art (two literature sweeps, 2026-09-15)

Context: a proposed experiment on cross-language, LLM-mediated discussion of an IETF draft (see draft-fengfar-led, "Dealing with LLMs in IETF Discussions"). The draft's authors asked for a design, an evaluation, and a check of the state of the art. This page merges two literature sweeps, both dated 2026-09-15. Sweep 1 covered general LLM-text reception (68 entries, 2 of them unresolved phantoms). Sweep 2 covered the LLM->human boundary across psycholinguistics, pragmatics, mind perception and applied fields (86 entries, 2 internal duplicates merged). Raw reports with full citations: [sweep/](sweep/) (seven subagent reports plus two retrieval manifests). Retrieved copies: [papers/](papers/); an entry that names a file in brackets has a copy there. Summaries are in short technical English. Inside each topical section, entries from 2024 and later come first, then older work.

## Short answer

Nobody has run this experiment: a human reading a live, real-stakes disagreement translated by an LLM from another human's own words. Related work exists on every piece of it. Readers cannot reliably detect AI-authored text, and their folk heuristics for detecting it are worse than chance (Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman 2023). Readability ratings and stated comprehension do not track measured comprehension or retention; fluency and a confident tone produce a felt understanding that outruns it (Kumar et al. 2026; Guerberof-Arenas & Toral 2020). An AI label lowers trust and perceived sincerity by itself, on unchanged text, and the drop is steepest in emotionally loaded genres such as condolence and health communication (Dorigoni & Giardino 2025; Lim, Schneider, Grover et al. 2025). Blinding the reader to authorship can flip or erase the gap entirely, which means much of the literature is measuring the label, not the text (Van Veen et al. 2024; Garcia, Qian & Palminteri 2026). LLM prose is measurably more uniform in predictability and narrower in vocabulary and syntax than human prose at corpus scale, though eye-tracking studies have not yet shown this converts cleanly into faster or easier reading (Basani & Chen 2025; Bolliger et al. 2025). Cross-language LLM mediation of a real argument between two people who cannot check each other's language has not been studied at all.

## Why LLM -> human differs from human -> human

1. Readers cannot detect AI authorship, and their folk heuristics for trying are actively wrong; almost every downstream effect below is triggered by disclosed or suspected authorship, not by any textual signature (Jakesch, Hancock & Naaman 2023; Garcia, Qian & Palminteri 2026).
2. LLM output is measurably lower in local surprisal and narrower in lexical and syntactic entropy than matched human text, which predicts easier per-word processing under surprisal theory (Basani & Chen 2025; Guo et al. 2024; Muñoz-Ortiz et al. 2024; Zamaraeva et al. 2025; Hale 2001; Levy 2008).
3. That evenness may not be the right kind of uniform. Human uniform information density is a discourse-structured smoothing, not flatness; LLM text can be locally low-surprisal while still lacking the structured non-uniformity that discourse organization imposes, a candidate source of the "off" feeling attentive readers report (Jaeger 2010; Tsipidi et al. 2024).
4. This conflicts with direct eye-tracking evidence. The largest purpose-built corpus of reading measures on LLM text found minimal variation across generating models and decoding strategies, unlike the individual and item variation typical of human-text corpora, so the predicted "easier reading" from lower surprisal has not shown up cleanly in gaze data (Bolliger et al. 2025, against the corpus-entropy studies above).
5. LLM text under-reproduces specific discourse-processing patterns. It misses the expectation-reversal effect human readers show for connective words (Britton et al. 2024), and it under-uses the concrete, imageable vocabulary that supports mental imagery and memorability (Seals & Shalin 2023).
6. Fluency is dissociable from comprehension and from felt quality. Confident, clean-syntax text produces a felt understanding that outruns measured understanding (Kumar et al. 2026), and a felt-empathy gap can survive even when a reader cannot tell which text is AI-authored from wording alone (Samardzic et al. 2025).
7. No committed speaker stands behind LLM speaker-meaning. Grice's cooperative principle and plan-based speech-act theory both presuppose an intending agent; formal linguistic competence can run without the functional and intentional machinery those theories assume, so fluent, coherent LLM text can still read as unauthored (Grice 1975; Cohen & Perrault 1979; Mahowald et al. 2024).
8. Grounding and repair are structurally different. Human dialogue repairs trouble through a tightly-timed, jointly-costed process; AI-mediated exchanges substitute re-prompting for that joint repair work (Clark & Brennan 1991; Dingemanse et al. 2015).
9. The human sender keeps public accountability for AI-assisted text even after the private sense of authorship collapses, and readers still over-credit the named sender's character (Draxler et al. 2023; Zhu & Molnár 2025).
10. Mind perception splits along two axes. LLM text tends to keep or gain perceived competence (Agency) but lose perceived warmth (Experience) once attributed, and discomfort peaks when machine-attributed text reads as too emotionally attuned (Gray, Gray & Wegner 2007; Gray & Wegner 2012).
11. The AI-label effect is not fixed. It is large in condolence, apology, dating-trust and complex-topic news, small or absent in negotiation tone and trust-building messages, and it can reverse when a visible AI absorbs blame for a failed interaction instead of authoring a success (Hohenstein & Jung 2020; Purcell, Jakesch et al. 2025; Hagedorn, Klinger & Sassenberg 2026; Lermann Henestrosa & Kimmerle 2024).
12. Blinded, objective-outcome studies conflict sharply with labelled, rating-based studies. Blinded expert readers and blinded patients rate LLM text as equal or better on comprehension, empathy and quality; the same content loses on every dimension once a reader is told or believes it is AI-authored (Van Veen et al. 2024; Ayers et al. 2023; Ovsyannikova et al. 2025, against Dorigoni & Giardino 2025 and Proksch et al. 2024).

## Gaps the literature leaves open

- The unaided receiver has never been the measured party.
- Every prior design mediates one direction only.
- Sealed originals revealed at the end appear in no communication study found.
- Most AI-mediated-communication studies measure trust and perception. Few measure receiver comprehension.
- Standards-body and mailing-list reactions to AI prose have no peer-reviewed study.
- The cost as a transfer from sender to receiver is a framing found nowhere.
- No study puts a human reader in front of an LLM translation of another real human's argument during a live disagreement; every translation-reception study uses prepared, non-contested text.
- Blinding to authorship is rarely combined with an objective outcome measure. Most blinded studies use rating scales; most objective-outcome studies are unblinded and labelled.
- The interaction between cross-language mediation and the AI-disclosure label is unstudied. Every disclosure study found is monolingual, and every cross-language study is silent on disclosure.
- Eye-tracking evidence and corpus-entropy evidence disagree on whether LLM text reads differently at the word level. No study reconciles the two on the same texts.
- Source memory for reading, not producing, another person's LLM-mediated text is untested. The one memory study found measures memory for content the reader helped create.

## What a design must respect (input for the next step)

- Use comprehension probes that need a specific fact from the message. Keep ratings as the comparison.
- Blind the receiver to AI involvement until the end. The reveal itself changes ratings.
- Fix the genre: comments on one draft. Genre flips the result.
- Track whether shared terms stabilise across turns.
- Log the sender's cost: time and rounds spent on the outgoing translation.
- Do not rely on back-translation. Check the revealed originals with a bilingual reader.
- Measure the AI-label effect separately from the text-quality effect. Run a labelled arm and a blinded arm on the same texts.
- Use objective outcomes (comprehension score, edit rate, task success), not only Likert ratings. Ratings and behavior diverge.
- Hash or timestamp the original text before translation, so a sealed original can be revealed and checked later without dispute.
- Track term drift across the discussion, not only at one back-translation snapshot.
- Measure reader effort (time, eye movements, self-paced reading, or a comprehension probe under time pressure), not only error rate. A text can be error-free and still cost more to read.
- Log the sender's cost and the receiver's assumed effort. Perceived effort mediates perceived creativity and quality independent of content.

## Instruments seen

Comprehension multiple-choice questions with McNemar or chi-square tests, cloze tests and reading time. Eye tracking and pupillometry. Likert trust, competence and quality scales with Cohen's d. Between-subjects label swap. Director/matcher tasks and lexical-entrainment counts. Back-translation agreement. Excess-vocabulary, tricolon, em-dash and stylometric-clustering forensics. Surprisal and uniform-information-density measures derived from language-model probabilities, compared against reading times. Source-memory tests for authorship attribution. Agency/Experience mind-perception scales and the Machine Heuristic Scale. Comprehension probes with objective scoring paired with a blinded label manipulation. Edit-rate and re-prompt rate as behavioral trust signals, distinct from stated trust. HPSG and other formal-grammar parsing for structural, not just lexical, divergence. Semantic-drift and structural-convergence measures for cross-author homogeneity.
